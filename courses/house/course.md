# Twig's Tinker School of House Keeping: Running Your House Without Losing Your Marbles in 11 Weeks

## Welcome to the Workshop

*"Gooood evening, and welcome to the only cleaning workshop hosted by a gnome who once let a sandwich fossilize inside a toolbox. A house is a machine, apprentice. It has moving parts, filters, seals and drains, and like any contraption it runs fine with a little upkeep and makes alarming noises without it. Nobody here is going to boss you around. I'm going to show you how the machine works, what each part needs, and why. What you do with that is your call. Great Scott, let's tinker with a house!"* — Twig

This workshop assumes **zero** experience. If nobody ever showed you how to mop, what a grout line is, or why the dryer has a little screen in it, you're in the right place. Nothing here is "obvious," and nothing here is a test of character.

It's written for brains that are **ADHD**, **autistic**, or both (**AuDHD**, as a lot of us say), and/or wired for **pathological demand avoidance (PDA)**. That changes how it's built:

- **Every task comes with a reason.** You get the *why* and *what happens if you skip it*, so the house's needs make sense instead of feeling like rules from nowhere.
- **No "shoulds."** Frequencies are what the machine needs to keep running, not orders. You're the operator. Operators decide.
- **Every task has a "Done looks like" line**, so you know exactly where the finish is and can stop there.
- **Three sizes for everything:** **Crumb** (the 2-minute version for low-battery days), **Standard**, and **Great Scott** (the full deep version for hyperfocus days). All three count.
- **The weeks can go in any order.** Jump to whatever's bugging you. The progress board doesn't care about order, and neither does Twig.
- **The checklists wipe themselves clean** every day, week or month. No streaks, no scores, no red marks. Unticked items just wait.

**The house this workshop was written for:** any house, apartment or rental, with any mix of people and pets. The examples assume a busy household with animals (dogs, cats, reptiles, maybe backyard birds), and they flag the climate bits (hard water, dust, heat, humidity, storm season, scorpion country) so you can keep the ones that fit where you live and skip the rest.

```tip
**This workshop isn't about a "clean house."** It's about a *working* house: one where you can find a fork, the air filter isn't choking the AC, nobody gets Salmonella, and the dryer doesn't catch fire. Pretty is optional. Working is the goal.
```

## How This Workshop Talks to You

*"A word on how I'll talk, apprentice. I make offers, not orders. When I say 'the fridge coils need cleaning about twice a year,' that's the fridge talking, not me. The fridge is very bossy. I'm just translating. Heh."* — Twig

### The rules the workshop follows

1. **Reasons first.** Every task says why it exists in plain mechanical terms: what builds up, what breaks, what smells, what makes someone sick.
2. **Natural consequences, not threats.** You'll see "If skipped:" lines. They describe what the house does if a task waits, like a manual describing what happens if you never change the oil. Some skips are harmless. Some are fire. You'll know which is which.
3. **Exact instructions.** Amounts, temperatures, order of steps, and what "done" looks like. No "just give it a quick clean" mystery phrases.
4. **Choices everywhere.** Bench Tasks are menus. Checklists are menus. The order of weeks is a menu. Pick, skip, swap.
5. **Safety items are flagged clearly.** A few things in a house really matter (fire, poison gas, germs from animals, electricity). Those get a ⚠ and a plain explanation. Everything else is flexible.

### The three sizes

| Size | When it fits | Example: the kitchen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Crumb** | Low battery, overwhelmed, sick, or "I don't wanna" is loud | Throw away one handful of trash. That's it. That counts |
| **Standard** | A normal day | Dishes done, counters wiped, trash out |
| **Great Scott** | Hyperfocus, novelty energy, or you're just in the mood | Pull everything off the counters, degrease the range hood filter, scrub the sink, clean the fridge |

The Crumb version exists because **starting is the hard part**, and a Crumb often turns into more on its own. If it doesn't, it still counts.

### When the "I don't want to" wall shows up

For PDA brains especially, the moment something feels like a demand, even a demand you made to yourself, the brain can slam the door. That's not laziness; it's a threat response. Some ways around the door, all optional:

- **Change the frame.** It's not "cleaning the bathroom," it's an experiment: *how long does it actually take to clean a toilet?* Time it. Science doesn't care if you finish.
- **Make it a side quest.** "Find every cup in the house" is a scavenger hunt. "Hunt for glowing pet stains (or scorpions, where you have them) with a blacklight" is legitimately fun (Week 10).
- **Shrink it below the threshold.** "I'll just get the gloves out." Often that's enough to start; sometimes it isn't, and that's fine too.
- **Borrow a body.** *Body doubling*: someone else in the room (in person, on a call, or a "clean with me" video) makes starting much easier for many ADHD brains.
- **Swap the order.** Every list in this workshop can be done in any order. Start with the part you mind least.
- **Give it to the machine.** Timers, alarms and smart-home reminders are the house asking, not a person. Some brains push back less against a beep than a voice.
- **Drop it.** Seriously. If today is not the day, the dishes will still be there tomorrow. Mess is not a moral failing. It's just stuff that hasn't been moved yet.

### Sensory toolkit

Cleaning is a sensory minefield: smells, textures, wet sleeves, noise, slime. You're allowed to engineer around all of it.

| Problem | Fixes |
| --- | --- |
| **Wet or slimy textures** | Nitrile gloves (buy the right size; loose gloves are worse), long-handled brushes so your hands never touch the gunk, a dish wand instead of a sponge |
| **Microfiber feels awful** | Wear gloves when using it, or switch to cotton rags or paper towels for dry work |
| **Strong smells** | Unscented or "free and clear" products, open windows, a fan blowing out. Many scents are also bad for cats and reptiles (Week 1), so unscented wins twice |
| **Noise (vacuum, disposal)** | Earplugs or noise-canceling headphones; a quieter cordless stick vacuum for daily passes |
| **Wet sleeves and cold water** | Short sleeves or arm bands, warm water, a waterproof apron |
| **Kneeling and bending** | Knee pads or a garden kneeler, long-handled tools, a stool |
| **Visual overwhelm** | Work one zone (a single counter, one shelf). Literally cover the rest with a sheet if it helps |
| **Boredom** | Podcast, audiobook, music, a game timer, or a "how fast can I do this" race |

### Week 1: The Cleaning Kit and the Chemistry That Bites

- What's actually in a cleaning kit, and what each thing is for
- Cleaning vs sanitizing vs disinfecting (they're different jobs)
- ⚠ The never-mix list
- The four forces of cleaning, and why "let it sit" is a real step
- Products and the animals: what's rough on cats, reptiles and birds
- **Bench Task menu (pick any, or none):** build one cleaning caddy; read the labels on everything under the sink and pull out anything with a warning you didn't know about; make a "never-mix" sign for the cabinet door.

#### Shop Manual: read this first

*"Every gnome knows the difference between a wrench and a hammer, apprentice, and every cleaner knows the difference between a degreaser and a disinfectant. Use the wrong one and you're just moving dirt around with extra steps. Or, in a couple of cases, making poison gas. Let's not do that one."* — Twig

**1. The kit.** You don't need much. This is the whole starter set, and most of it is cheap.

| Tool | What it's for | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nitrile gloves** | Protecting your hands; avoiding gross textures | Get the right size. Keep a box in the kitchen and one in the bathroom |
| **Microfiber cloths** (or cotton rags) | Wiping, dusting, polishing | Wash them without fabric softener; softener coats the fibers and they stop grabbing dirt. Color-code: one color for toilets only |
| **Scrub brush + old toothbrush** | Grout, corners, faucets | A long-handled brush keeps your hands out of the gunk |
| **Non-scratch scrub sponge** | Sinks, tubs, stovetops | Replace or microwave-sanitize often (Week 5) |
| **Spray bottles, labeled** | Your mixes | Label *every* bottle with what's in it. Mystery bottles are how mixing accidents happen |
| **Bucket** | Mopping, soaking | One for floors, a separate one for animal gear (Week 10) |
| **Mop** | Hard floors | A flat microfiber mop with washable pads is easiest to learn |
| **Broom + dustpan**, or a stick vacuum | Floors | With dogs and cats, a vacuum with a HEPA filter is worth it |
| **Squeegee** | Windows, shower glass | Cuts window cleaning time in half (Week 4) |
| **Dish soap** (plain, unscented if possible) | The universal degreaser | A few drops in warm water cleans most surfaces in the house |
| **All-purpose cleaner** | Counters, appliances, sticky stuff | Or your own mix (Week 11) |
| **Baking soda** | Gentle scrubbing powder, deodorizing | Mild abrasive; won't scratch most surfaces |
| **White vinegar (5%)** | Hard-water spots, glass, descaling | An acid. Great on hard-water mineral crust. Not a disinfectant (see below) |
| **An EPA-registered disinfectant** or plain unscented bleach | Killing germs where it matters | Toilets, raw-meat areas, animal habitats |
| **Enzymatic cleaner** | Pet urine, vomit, poop stains | The only thing that truly removes urine odor (Week 10) |

**The caddy trick.** Put one set (gloves, cloth, spray, scrub brush) in a carry caddy **in each room you clean most** (kitchen, bathroom). If the tools are already *there*, you skip the "go find the stuff" step, and for an ADHD brain that step is often where the whole task dies.

**2. Cleaning, sanitizing and disinfecting are three different jobs.**

| Job | What it does | What you use | When it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cleaning** | Physically removes dirt and most germs | Soap or detergent, water, scrubbing | Almost everything, almost always |
| **Sanitizing** | Lowers germs to a safer level | Sanitizer per label (or very hot water, like a dishwasher) | Food surfaces, cutting boards |
| **Disinfecting** | Kills most germs on a surface | An **EPA-registered disinfectant**, or bleach diluted per label | Toilets, raw meat spills, animal habitats and gear, sick-household surfaces |

**Clean first, then disinfect.** Disinfectant can't reach germs hiding under grime. Wipe off the gunk, *then* disinfect.

**Vinegar is a cleaner, not a disinfectant.** It's excellent at dissolving mineral scale and cutting soap scum, and it kills some germs, but it isn't registered with the EPA as a disinfectant and doesn't meet that standard. For the reptile tank or the raw chicken spill, use a real disinfectant.

**3. The basic bleach disinfecting mix** (for when you need it): about **20 mL regular, unscented household bleach per 1 L of cool water**, in a labeled bottle. Make it fresh each day you use it (it weakens fast once diluted). **Bleach strengths vary, and the label wins:** if your bottle gives its own disinfecting dilution, use that. Leave it wet on the surface for at least 1 minute (or the label's time), then rinse surfaces that animals or food will touch, and let it air dry.

```stop Stop: the never-mix list
**4. ⚠ The never-mix list.** This is the one part of the workshop that's genuinely non-negotiable, because the house can't undo it. Mixing these makes toxic gas, sometimes in seconds, and bathrooms are small rooms.
```

| Never mix | What it makes | Where people get caught |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bleach + ammonia** | Chloramine gas | Glass cleaners often contain ammonia. **Urine contains ammonia**: bleach on pet pee or in a litter box or toilet with urine in it can do this |
| **Bleach + any acid** (vinegar, lemon juice, many toilet-bowl and rust cleaners) | Chlorine gas | "Natural" citrus cleaner followed by bleach spray in the shower |
| **Bleach + rubbing alcohol** | Chloroform and other nasties | Disinfecting "harder" by layering products |
| **Hydrogen peroxide + vinegar in the same bottle** | Peracetic acid (corrosive) | Pre-mixing "super cleaner" sprays |
| **Two different drain cleaners** | Heat, spattering, toxic fumes | Second bottle when the first "didn't work" |

**The simple rule:** **bleach only ever gets mixed with water.** One product at a time. Rinse with water between products. Keep everything in its original or a clearly labeled bottle. If you ever mix something by accident and smell something sharp: **leave the room, get fresh air, and call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222** (24/7, free).

**5. The four forces of cleaning (TACT).** Every cleaning job uses four forces. If one is weak, turn up another.

| Force | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **T**ime | Let the cleaner sit ("dwell time") | Spray the stovetop, walk away for 5 minutes, then wipe: the grease slides off instead of needing a fight |
| **A**ction | Scrubbing, agitation | A brush on grout |
| **C**hemistry | The right product for the dirt | Acid for mineral scale, soap for grease, enzymes for urine |
| **T**emperature | Warm water works faster | Warm, not boiling, for most jobs |

*"Time is the lazy gnome's secret weapon, apprentice. Spray, walk away, come back. The chemistry does the scrubbing while you go look at your phone. Heh."* — Twig

**Match the chemistry to the dirt:**

| Dirt | What it is chemically | What dissolves it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grease, body oil, food film | Fats (acidic-ish) | Alkaline: dish soap, degreaser, baking soda |
| White crust, water spots, scale | Minerals (alkaline) | Acid: vinegar, citric acid, lemon |
| Soap scum | Soap + minerals | Acid, then soap |
| Urine, vomit, blood, poop stains | Proteins and organic matter | Enzymatic cleaner (cold water for blood) |
| Mold and mildew | Living fungus | Scrub with soap, then disinfectant or a mold-labeled product; fix the moisture |
| Rust | Iron oxide | Rust remover (often an acid; never near bleach) |

**6. Two cleaning rules that save work.**

- **Top to bottom.** Dust falls. Clean ceiling fans and shelves *before* the floor, or you'll do the floor twice.
- **Dry to wet.** Dust and vacuum *before* wiping or mopping. Wet dust turns into mud.

**7. Products and the animals.** Pets have very different chemistry from people, and from each other.

- **Cats** can't break down some compounds that dogs and people handle fine. **Phenols** (found in some disinfectants and pine-oil cleaners) and many **essential oils** (tea tree, eucalyptus, citrus oils, peppermint, pine, and more) can poison cats, through licking paws, skin contact or breathing diffused oil. Keep cats out until surfaces are **dry**, and favor unscented products.
- **Reptiles** (tortoises, turtles, lizards, snakes) breathe close to the surface they live on and absorb things through their skin and shell. Their habitats get **unscented** cleaners only, rinsed well and dried fully (Week 10).
- **Birds** have very sensitive lungs. Backyard chickens and ducks live outside, which helps, but don't spray aerosols near any birds, indoor or out, or clean their gear with strong fumes in a closed space.
- **Dogs** are more forgiving than cats but still lick floors and paws: let floors dry before they walk on them.

```tip
**Rule of paw:** unscented, one product at a time, rinse surfaces animals touch, and keep everyone out until it's dry. That covers 90% of pet safety in cleaning. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control line is (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply).
```

### Week 2: Systems for Brains That Do Their Own Thing

#### Shop Manual: read this first

*"A brilliant inventor doesn't rely on willpower, apprentice. Willpower is a terrible power source: unreliable, runs out by lunch, and sulks. A brilliant inventor builds the contraption so the right thing is the easy thing. That's what this week is: engineering, not character."* — Twig

**1. Tidying and cleaning are two different jobs.**

| | **Tidying** | **Cleaning** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Putting stuff where it lives | Removing dirt, grime and germs |
| Example | Dishes from the couch to the sink | Washing the dishes |
| Needs | Decisions ("where does this go?") | Tools and chemistry |
| Hard part for ADHD brains | All the tiny decisions | Starting, and the boredom |

You often can't clean until you've tidied (you can't wipe a counter buried in mail). When everything feels like too much, **tidying first** gives the fastest visible change.

**2. The Five Things method** (from KC Davis). Every mess in any room is made of only five kinds of things. Deal with them in this order, one kind at a time:

1. **Trash.** Bag in hand, sweep the room for anything that's garbage. Just trash, nothing else.
2. **Dishes.** Gather every cup, plate and fork into one pile or basin. Take them to the kitchen. (You don't have to wash them yet.)
3. **Laundry.** Every clothing item, towel and blanket into one basket.
4. **Things with a place.** Put each one where it lives.
5. **Things without a place.** Make a pile. Either give them a home, or put them in a "decide later" bin. That bin is allowed to exist.

Why it works: one category at a time means **one kind of decision at a time**, so your brain doesn't have to switch tracks every three seconds. You also see progress fast, and progress is fuel. You can stop after any step.

**3. Rig the house so it does half the work.**

- **Fewer steps wins.** Every step between you and done (open a cabinet, lift a lid, walk to another room) is a place to stall. Open bins beat lidded ones. Hooks beat hangers. A trash can in every room beats one trash can in the kitchen.
- **Store things where they're used.** Bathroom cleaner lives in the bathroom. Dog leash lives by the door. Duplicates are fine; a second $4 scrub brush saves a hundred trips.
- **Visible beats hidden.** "Out of sight, out of mind" is literal for a lot of ADHD brains. Clear bins, open shelves and labels mean you can see what you have.
- **Landing zones.** A tray or bowl by the door for keys, wallet, sunglasses and mail. Things land there instead of everywhere.
- **The doom box.** A single box or basket where unsorted stuff can go *temporarily*. Contained chaos beats spread-out chaos. Go through it when you have the energy.
- **Reduce the dishes.** Fewer cups and plates in circulation means the pile can't get as big. Paper plates on hard days are a legitimate tool, not a failure.

**4. Triggers and timers.**

- **Attach tasks to things you already do** ("habit stacking"): wipe the bathroom sink after brushing your teeth; empty the dishwasher while the coffee brews; scoop the litter when you feed the cats.
- **Timers make tasks finite.** "Clean until the timer goes off" (10 or 15 minutes) is easier to start than "clean the kitchen," because there's a guaranteed end. When it beeps, you're allowed to stop, even mid-wipe.
- **The closing shift.** Restaurants do a closing routine every night so the morning crew walks into a ready kitchen. A 10-minute closing shift (dishes in the dishwasher, counters clear, tomorrow's stuff in the landing zone) is a gift to Morning You.
- **Let the house ask.** Recurring reminders (phone, calendar, smart home) for the monthly and yearly items. Write the reminder as information, not an order: "AC filter: last changed Oct 3" instead of "CHANGE THE FILTER."

```tip
**Smart-home tie-in (optional):** if you run a smart-home dashboard (Home Assistant or similar), a "House Machine" card listing each maintenance item with its last-done date, turning amber when it's getting due and red when it's past. Information on a screen, no nagging.
```

**5. How to read the checklists in the Toolbox.**

- They're **menus**. Nothing on them is required on any given day.
- They **reset themselves** (daily at midnight, weekly on Monday, monthly on the 1st). No streaks, no history of what you missed.
- Items are **ordered by consequence**: the ones near the top cause real problems if they wait (fire, germs, smells, pests). The ones near the bottom are nice-to-haves.
- Each item has a **why** so you can decide for yourself whether today's the day.

#### Shop Manual: read this first

*"Floors are where everything ends up, apprentice: dust, hair, crumbs, mud, whatever the pets brought in or dropped. Gravity is relentless. The good news is floors are also the easiest place to see a difference fast."* — Twig

**1. Know your floors.** Walk the house and figure out what each floor is. If you can't tell, a corner inside a closet usually shows the edge of the material.

| Floor | How to recognize it | Biggest enemy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ceramic or porcelain tile** | Hard, cold, with grout lines between tiles | Dirty grout; standing on grime |
| **Natural stone** (travertine, marble, slate) | Tile with natural variation, often slightly porous or pitted | **Acids** (vinegar, lemon) etch it permanently |
| **Vinyl plank / LVP / sheet vinyl** | Looks like wood or tile but feels slightly soft and warm; plastic-y edge | Steam and soaking water at the seams; harsh scrubbing |
| **Hardwood** | Real wood grain, can dent with a fingernail | Water sitting on it; grit scratching the finish |
| **Laminate** | Photo of wood on a hard click-together board; edges look printed | Water getting into seams (it swells and never goes back) |
| **Sealed concrete** | Smooth, gray or stained, common in hot climates | Not much; very forgiving |
| **Carpet and rugs** | Soft | Hair, dander, embedded grit, urine |

**2. Vacuuming and sweeping.**

| Task | How often | Why | If skipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vacuum or sweep high-traffic paths (kitchen, hallway, by doors) | **2–3× a week with pets**; daily during heavy shedding | Grit acts like sandpaper on every floor finish. Pet hair and dander feed dust mites and allergies | Dull, scratched floors; hair tumbleweeds; itchy humans |
| Vacuum whole house, including under furniture edges | Weekly | Dust and hair migrate to edges and corners | Gray fuzz lines along baseboards |
| Vacuum under furniture, beds, couches | Monthly | Hidden dust, lost toys, and a hiding spot for scorpions and crickets | Pests set up camp |

**How to vacuum (it's not obvious):**

1. Pick up anything that'll get sucked in: cords, socks, small toys, rug fringe.
2. Start at the far side of the room and work toward the door so you don't walk over clean floor.
3. **Slow passes.** Pushing slowly lets the brush roll and suction actually lift dirt. Fast passes mostly make noise.
4. Overlap each stroke a little, like mowing a lawn.
5. Use the crevice tool along baseboards once a week or so.
6. **Done looks like:** no visible hair, crumbs or grit when you look across the floor at a low angle (shine a flashlight sideways along the floor; it reveals everything).

**Vacuum care** (this matters more with pets): empty the bin or bag when it's half to two-thirds full (suction drops as it fills); **cut hair off the brush roll** with scissors monthly (hair wraps around it and stops it spinning); wash or replace filters per the manual.

**3. Mopping.**

| Task | How often | Why | If skipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mop kitchen floor | Weekly, plus spills same day | Food drips and grease make a sticky film that grabs more dirt | Sticky floor, ants |
| Mop bathroom floor, especially around the toilet | Weekly | Urine splash. Yes, even if nobody admits it | Smell that doesn't go away |
| Mop the rest of the hard floors | Every 2–4 weeks | Dust film and paw prints | Dull, gritty floors |

**The two-bucket method:** one bucket of clean solution, one of rinse water. Dip mop in solution, mop a small area, rinse mop in the rinse bucket, wring, repeat. This keeps you from just spreading dirty water around. With a flat microfiber mop, swap to a fresh pad when the old one looks gray instead.

**The rule of damp, not wet.** For almost every floor, the mop should be **damp**, not dripping. Wring it until it barely drips. Standing water seeps into seams, grout and wood, and in a house with animals it makes slippery floors.

**Basic floor solution:** about **4 L warm water + a few drops (around 5 mL) of plain dish soap**, or a pH-neutral floor cleaner per its label. More soap is **not** better: it leaves a sticky film that attracts dirt. If the floor feels tacky after drying, you used too much; mop once with plain water.

**4. Floor by floor.**

- **Tile and grout:** sweep or vacuum, then mop with soap solution. Grout is porous and soaks up dirt. To clean it: paste of baking soda and water on the grout lines, let sit 10 minutes, scrub with a stiff brush or toothbrush, rinse, wipe dry. **Seal grout about once a year** with a grout sealer from the hardware store: sealed grout resists stains and is much easier to keep clean.
- **Natural stone:** **never vinegar, lemon or acidic cleaners**, which permanently etch dull spots. Use a pH-neutral stone cleaner or plain water with a drop of dish soap.
- **Vinyl and LVP:** damp mop, pH-neutral cleaner. Skip steam mops unless the floor's manufacturer says yes (steam can loosen the glue and seams and void the warranty). Felt pads under furniture.
- **Hardwood:** dry dust mop or vacuum (hard-floor setting, brush roll off). Damp mop only with a wood-floor cleaner, wrung almost dry. Wipe up water and pet accidents right away. No steam, no soaking, no vinegar habit (it dulls the finish over time).
- **Laminate:** like hardwood: dry first, barely damp mop, no steam, no puddles.
- **Sealed concrete:** sweep, mop with soap solution. Reseal every few years if water stops beading on it.
- **Carpet and rugs:** vacuum (slowly, in two directions) as above. **Deep-clean with a carpet extractor** (rentable, or own a small spot-cleaner) **every 6–12 months**, and more often in pet zones. Treat stains right away: blot (don't rub) from the outside in. For pet urine, see Week 10.

```tip
**Pet hair trick:** put on a damp rubber dish glove and drag your hand across the rug or couch. The hair rolls up into clumps you can pick off. A rubber squeegee dragged across carpet does the same thing on a bigger scale, and it's weirdly satisfying.
```

**5. Dust.** Dry and windy places put a fine dust on everything, and a dust storm can coat the whole house in an afternoon. Things that help:

- **Doormats outside and inside every exterior door.** Most floor dirt walks in. A good mat catches a big share of it.
- **Shoes off at the door** (or house-only shoes). This also matters for the coop shoes (Week 10).
- **After a dust storm:** vacuum and dust before you mop (dry to wet), check the AC filter (Week 9), and wipe window sills.

#### Shop Manual: read this first

*"Up is where dust goes to hide, apprentice. Nobody looks up. The ceiling fan has a fur coat, the AC vents are fuzzy, and the top of the fridge is a time capsule. Start high, and gravity becomes your assistant instead of your enemy."* — Twig

**1. Up high.**

| Task | How often | Why | If skipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ceiling fan blades | Monthly (every 2 weeks in heavy-use summer) | Fans fling dust and hair around the room every time they spin | Dust "rain" when you turn it on; allergy flare |
| AC supply vents and return grilles | Monthly | Dusty vents blow dust back into the room; dusty returns choke airflow | Gray streaks around vents; the AC works harder |
| Light fixtures and bulbs | Every 3–6 months | Dust and dead bugs dim the light and can bake onto hot bulbs | Dimmer rooms; burnt-dust smell |
| Cobwebs in corners and ceilings | Monthly | Webs collect dust; webs also mean spiders, which mean bugs | Fuzzy corners |
| Top of fridge, cabinets, door frames | Every 1–3 months | Grease plus dust makes a sticky gray layer | Hard-to-remove gunk |

**The pillowcase trick for fans:** slide an old pillowcase over one blade, press it gently against the top and bottom, and pull it back. The dust stays *inside* the pillowcase instead of raining on you. Shake it out outside, then wash it. Turn the fan **off** first. (Yes, obviously. Twig has seen things.)

**Fan direction:** in summer, fans should spin **counterclockwise** (looking up) to push air down for a wind-chill effect; in winter, clockwise on low to gently push warm air down from the ceiling. There's usually a small switch on the motor housing. Hot climates get most of the year on counterclockwise.

**2. Walls.** Most walls just need occasional dusting and **spot cleaning** when something splashes. A full wall wash is a once-a-year (or move-out) thing, or for kitchens with grease.

**First, know your paint.** The shinier the paint, the more scrubbing it can take.

| Finish | Looks like | Scrubbing tolerance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Flat / matte** | No shine at all | Low: rubbing can leave shiny spots or take paint off. Gentle dab with a barely damp cloth |
| **Eggshell / satin** | Soft glow | Medium: soft sponge with mild soap is fine |
| **Semi-gloss / gloss** | Shiny (common in kitchens, bathrooms and trim) | High: can take a real wipe-down |

**Always test** on a hidden spot (behind a door, inside a closet) first.

**Spot cleaning a wall:**

1. Dust or dry-wipe the spot first.
2. Warm water + a drop of dish soap on a soft sponge, wrung almost dry.
3. Wipe gently. For scuffs, a melamine sponge ("magic eraser") works, but it's a very fine abrasive: on flat paint, it can leave a dull or shiny patch. Test first.
4. Wipe with a clean damp cloth, then dry.

**Washing a whole wall:** dust it first (a dry microfiber mop works great), then **wash from the bottom up** and rinse from the top down. Why bottom up? Drips running down a dirty wall leave clean streaks that are hard to blend; drips running down an already-clean wall wipe off easily.

**3. High-touch zones.** These are the most germ-heavy surfaces in the house, because every hand touches them.

| Task | How often | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light switches, door handles, cabinet pulls, fridge handle, remotes, faucet handles | Weekly; daily during an illness in the house | Hands carry germs and skin oil; after handling animals, these are where Salmonella hitches a ride |
| Doors and door frames | Monthly | Hand prints, dog nose art, cat rubs |
| Baseboards | Monthly-ish | They're the dust shelf of the house, and pet hair gathers there |

**Baseboards without kneeling:** a microfiber mop or a damp cloth wrapped around a long-handled duster. Or put on knee pads and a podcast and make it the "scorpion inspection" (they hide in the gaps; Week 10).

**4. Windows.** **Strong sun and hard water** are the two window villains.

| Task | How often | Why | If skipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spot clean inside (nose and paw prints) | As noticed | Dogs and cats love windows | Smudges block the view |
| Full clean inside | Every 2–3 months | Film from cooking, dust and smoke | Hazy glass |
| Full clean outside, screens and tracks | Twice a year: spring and fall (after your storm season, if you have one) | Wind, dust and rain leave mud spots; tracks fill with grit, and grit wears out the rollers | Windows that stick; mud-freckled glass |

**The squeegee method:**

1. **Pick the time.** Clean windows **when they're in the shade** (early morning, or the side of the house the sun isn't on). In strong sun, the cleaner dries before you can squeegee it, and you get streaks every time.
2. **Dust first.** Wipe or vacuum the frame and sill so you're not making mud.
3. **Mix:** a bucket of warm water with a few drops of dish soap (about 5 mL in 4 L), or a spray bottle of half white vinegar, half water for mineral spots.
4. **Wet the glass** with a sponge or scrubber.
5. **Squeegee:** start at the top corner, pull straight down (or across in an S-shape), and **wipe the rubber blade on a dry cloth after every stroke**.
6. Wipe the edges and sill with a dry cloth.
7. **Done looks like:** no streaks when you look at the glass from an angle with light behind it.

**Hard water spots** (white rings from sprinklers or rain): spray full-strength white vinegar, let it sit 5 minutes, then scrub with a non-scratch pad. Stubborn spots may need a dedicated hard-water glass remover. Adjust sprinklers so they don't hit the glass, or the spots just come back.

**Screens:** take them out (they lift and pop out of the track), lay them on the ground outside, hose gently, scrub with a soft brush and soapy water, rinse, and let them dry in the sun before putting them back.

**Tracks:** vacuum loose grit with the crevice tool, then wipe with a damp cloth; an old toothbrush gets the corners. A dry silicone spray on the track afterward makes sliding doors glide.

**1. The kitchen.**

| Task | How often | Why | If skipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Wipe counters and stovetop | After cooking, or daily | Crumbs and drips feed ants and roaches; raw meat juice carries germs | Sticky surfaces, pests |

| Sanitize or replace sponges | Sponge: sanitize every few days, replace every 1–2 weeks | Sponges are warm, wet and full of food: a perfect germ farm | A sponge that spreads germs instead of removing them |

**Dishes** have their own week now: Week 6 covers the whole system, from scraping to putting away.

**Sponges:** to sanitize, wet the sponge (it must be **wet**; a dry sponge can catch fire) and microwave it on high for 1–2 minutes, or run it through the dishwasher on the hottest setting with a dry cycle. It'll be very hot: let it cool before touching. Dish brushes dry faster and grow fewer germs than sponges, and the dishwasher can clean them too.

**Cutting boards:** use a separate board for raw meat. Wash with hot soapy water after every use. Plastic boards can go in the dishwasher. Wood boards: wash by hand, dry standing up, and rub with food-grade mineral oil now and then; the lemon-and-salt scrub (Week 11) deodorizes them.

**Stovetop:**

- **Glass or ceramic top:** let it cool, wipe with a damp cloth. For burnt-on spots: sprinkle baking soda, lay a hot damp towel on top for 15 minutes, then wipe. Use a razor scraper held flat for anything left (most glass tops allow it; check the manual).
- **Gas grates and burners:** grates can soak in hot soapy water in the sink. Burner caps come off; make sure the little holes are clear and everything is **completely dry** before you put it back and light it.
- **Coil electric:** coils lift out to clean the drip pans underneath. Don't submerge the coils themselves.

**2. The bathroom.**

**How to clean a toilet** (in this order, so you never move germs onto clean parts):

1. Gloves on. Use the toilet-only cloth color.
2. **Bowl:** squirt toilet cleaner under the rim. Let it sit 5–10 minutes (dwell time). Don't add anything else to the bowl; toilet cleaners are often acidic, and **urine plus bleach is the chloramine combo** from Week 1. One product only.
3. While it sits: spray the **outside**, top to bottom: tank, handle, lid, seat (top and underside), the hinges, the outside of the bowl, and the base where it meets the floor.
4. Wipe the outside **from cleanest to dirtiest**: tank and handle, lid, seat, hinges, outside of bowl, base, and the floor around it last.
5. Scrub the bowl with the brush, under the rim especially. Flush.
6. Rinse the brush in the clean flush water and let it drip dry between the seat and bowl before putting it back.
7. **Done looks like:** no ring in the bowl, nothing yellow around the base or hinges, no smell.

**Hard water rings in the toilet:** turn off the valve behind the toilet and flush to drain the bowl, then scrub the ring with a pumice stone made for toilets (keep it wet) or a citric acid or vinegar soak. The ring is minerals, so acid dissolves it.

**Showerheads and faucet aerators** (hard water clogs them): fill a sandwich bag with white vinegar, slip it over the showerhead, and hold it on with a rubber band so the face is submerged. Leave it for an hour to overnight, then remove and run hot water. **Exception:** some finishes (brass, oil-rubbed bronze, some nickel) can be damaged by long acid soaks. For those, limit to 15–30 minutes or check the maker's care guide.

**Soap scum on tub and glass:** a spray of half vinegar and half warm water, sit 10 minutes, scrub, rinse. Or a paste of baking soda and dish soap. **Not on natural stone** (Week 3).

**3. Mold and mildew.**

- **Mildew** is surface mold: black, gray or pink spots on caulk, grout and the curtain. Scrub with soap and water, then spray a mold-labeled cleaner or the bleach mix from Week 1 (ventilate, gloves, never with vinegar). Rinse.
- **Fix the moisture or it comes back:** run the fan, squeegee, crack the door, wash the liner.
- **Mold in caulk** that won't scrub out is *in* the caulk. Replacing caulk is a beginner-friendly job (cut out the old, clean, dry, run a new bead).
- ⚠ **Mold larger than about 1 m² (10 ft²)**, mold in walls, a musty smell with no visible source, or mold after a leak: that's a moisture problem in the structure. EPA guidance is to bring in a professional for larger areas.

### Week 6: Dishes, Without the Dread

- Hard water: cloudy glasses, spots, film
- Shrinking the pile before it starts

#### Shop Manual: read this first

*"Dishes are the hydra of housework, apprentice: wash one, two more appear. But a hydra is just a monster with a supply-chain problem. Fix the supply chain and the monster gets a lot smaller. Also, I've seen your mug collection. We need to talk about the mug collection."* — Twig

**1. Why dishes pile up.** For AuDHD brains, dishes stack up for very specific, very fixable reasons:

| Snag | What's happening | Engineering fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Too many steps** | Scrape, rinse, load, run, unload, put away: six tasks pretending to be one | Treat each step as its own small job (the assembly line below) |
| **Gross textures** | Wet food, soggy bits, slimy water | Gloves, a dish wand, a silicone scraper, a drain basket you can lift out by the rim |
| **The unload bottleneck** | The dishwasher is full of clean dishes, so dirty ones pile in the sink | Unload while something else is happening (kettle boiling, coffee brewing) |
| **Delayed consequences** | Nothing bad happens for a day, then everything is crusty | Soak (Time does the scrubbing), and fewer dishes in circulation |
| **No clear "done"** | Is it done when they're washed? Dried? Put away? | The **Done looks like** line below |

**2. The dish assembly line.** Six stations. Doing *any one of them* moves the whole line forward, and you can stop after any station.

1. **Collect.** Every cup and plate from around the house goes to the kitchen. (Five Things, step 2.)
2. **Scrape.** Food into the trash or compost. A silicone spatula does it without your fingers touching anything.
3. **Stack and soak.** Like with like (plates with plates), and anything crusty gets hot water and a drop of soap. Walk away. Stacked dishes take up a third of the space and look a lot less scary.
4. **Wash.** Into the dishwasher, or the hand-wash basin.
5. **Dry.** Air dry in the rack or let the dishwasher finish. Air drying is more hygienic than a towel, and it's zero effort.
6. **Put away.** The step most likely to get skipped. Store everyday dishes within one step of the dishwasher or rack, so this takes 2 minutes, not 10.

**Done looks like:** the sink is empty (or holding only soaking pots), the counter is clear of dirty dishes, and the dishwasher is either running, empty, or partly loaded with dirty dishes (not clean ones).

**3. Loading a dishwasher.** The spray arms shoot water from below (and often the middle). Anything that blocks the spray or faces away from it stays dirty.

| Rack | What goes there | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bottom** | Plates, pots, pans, big bowls, sheet pans | Plates upright, **dirty side facing the center**. Pots face down. Big flat things along the sides or back so they don't block the spray |
| **Top** | Cups, glasses, small bowls, plastic containers, mugs | Upside down, angled so water drains off and doesn't pool in the bottoms. Plastic goes up top, away from the heating element |
| **Utensil basket** | Forks, spoons, spatulas | Mix them up (nesting spoons don't get clean). **Sharp knives point down** for safety, or better, hand-wash them |

**Run it full, not crammed.** If items touch or nest, water can't reach between them. **Scrape, don't rinse:** modern detergent uses enzymes that need some food to bite into, and pre-rinsing wastes water. Clean the filter monthly (Week 9).

**Keep out of the dishwasher:** cast iron (rusts and loses its seasoning), wood (cracks and warps), good kitchen knives (dulls them), most nonstick pans unless the label says dishwasher-safe, insulated bottles and travel mugs (the vacuum seal fails), aluminum (turns dark gray), crystal and hand-painted or gold-rimmed dishes, and anything glued together.

**4. Hand-washing.**

- **Setup:** a basin or one side of the sink with **hot soapy water**, as hot as your gloves are comfortable with (about 43–49 °C (110–120 °F) is typical). A second side or basin of rinse water, and a drying rack.
- **Order:** glasses → silverware → plates → pots and pans (cleanest to dirtiest, so the water stays useful longer). Change the water when it turns greasy or cool.
- **Tools:** a dish brush or wand (dries fast, grows fewer germs than a sponge), a non-scratch scrubber for pans, a bottle brush.
- **Rinse and air dry.** Done.

**5. The special cases.**

| Item | How | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cast iron** | Rinse with hot water, scrub with a brush (or the eggshell scrub from Week 11, or coarse salt). A little soap is fine on a well-seasoned pan. **Dry on a warm burner**, then rub in a few drops of oil | Water left on it rusts it; the oil layer is the nonstick surface |
| **Nonstick pans** | Soft sponge, no metal scrubbers or metal utensils | Scratches ruin the coating |
| ⚠ **Sharp knives** | Wash and dry **right away**, one at a time, blade pointing away from you. **Never leave a knife in a sink of soapy water** | A hidden blade under the bubbles is how people cut their hands |
| **Wooden spoons and boards** | Hand-wash, dry standing up, oil now and then (food-grade mineral oil). Lemon and salt to deodorize (Week 11) | Soaking cracks and warps wood |
| **Insulated bottles and travel mugs** | Bottle brush, and **take the lid apart**: the rubber gasket pops out | Mold grows under the gasket where you can't see it |
| **Blender** | Half fill with warm water and a drop of soap, blend 30 seconds, rinse | Cleans the blades without your fingers going near them |
| **Graters and garlic presses** | Scrub right after use with a brush; soak if dried | Dried-on bits are like concrete |

**6. Hard water.** Hard water leaves minerals on everything it dries on.

- **Spots and film:** use a **rinse aid** in the dishwasher (it helps water sheet off instead of drying in droplets).
- **Cloudy glasses:** soak one in white vinegar for 5 minutes. If it comes out clear, it was **mineral film**, and a monthly vinegar or citric-acid cycle (Week 9) fixes it. If it's still cloudy, it's **etching**: permanent, microscopic scratches, usually from too much detergent with soft water or very hot cycles. Etching can't be undone; use a little less detergent going forward.

**7. Shrink the pile before it starts.**

- **Fewer dishes in circulation.** Box up half the cups and plates for a week. The pile can only get as big as the cupboard lets it.
- **One cup per person**, each a different color, refilled all day instead of a new one each drink.
- **Paper plates on hard days** are a tool, not a failure.
- **One-pot and sheet-pan meals** mean one thing to wash.
- **Soak right away.** The 30 seconds of filling a pan with hot water right after cooking saves 10 minutes of scrubbing tomorrow.

| Task | How often | Why | If skipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dishes washed or dishwasher run | Daily | In a warm house, food residue grows bacteria in hours and dries on hard | Smell, fruit flies, ants, crusted pans |
| Dishwasher unloaded | Whenever it's clean | A full clean dishwasher blocks the whole line | Dirty dishes pile in the sink |
| Dish brush or sponge cleaned or replaced | Sponge: sanitize every few days, replace every 1–2 weeks; brush: dishwasher weekly | They're warm, wet and full of food | They spread germs instead of removing them |
| Drying rack and drip tray washed | Weekly | Standing water grows slime and mildew | Pink or black gunk in the tray |
| Dishwasher filter and cleaning cycle | Monthly (Week 9) | Food bits and mineral film | Gritty dishes, smell |

**The three sizes:**

- **Crumb:** fill one crusty pan with hot water and walk away. Or put three things in the dishwasher.
- **Standard:** the whole assembly line, once.
- **Great Scott:** every dish in the house done, the drying rack scrubbed, the dishwasher filter cleaned, and half the mugs retired to the donation box.

### Week 7: Laundry, the Five-Stage Loop

- The laundry loop, and the two places it always breaks
- Reading care labels (the little symbols)
- Sorting the lazy-genius way
- Detergent, load size and water temperature
- Stains: what to use on what
- Pet, coop and animal laundry
- Drying, the sun, and dryer sheets vs dryer balls
- Folding is optional: no-fold systems that actually work
- How often to wash what
- **Bench Task menu:** set a "laundry's done" alarm (or a smart-home notification) for your next load; put a hamper wherever clothes actually land; try one load with half the detergent you normally use; set up one no-fold bin.

#### Shop Manual: read this first

*"Laundry isn't one chore, apprentice. It's a conveyor belt with five stations, and the belt stops dead at two of them: the washer that finished three hours ago, and the clean pile on the chair that's become a load-bearing structure. We're not going to make you love folding. We're going to make folding optional. Great Scott, the liberation!"* — Twig

**1. The loop.** Laundry runs in five stages: **Collect → Wash → Dry → Fold (or not) → Put away.** It almost always breaks at the **transfers**:

| Break point | What happens | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Washer → dryer** | Wet clothes sit and sour. After several hours, they can smell musty and need re-washing | A phone timer set the moment you start the washer, or a smart-home alert (below). Only start a load when you'll be home for the transfer |
| **Dryer → put away** | The clean pile becomes furniture | No-fold bins and hanging straight from the dryer (section 7) |

```tip
**Smart-home tie-in (optional):** a smart plug with power monitoring on the washer (or a vibration sensor on a gas dryer or a 240 V machine that can't use a plug). When power drops to near zero for a couple of minutes, the cycle's done: a smart speaker announces it, or your phone pings. "The washer has finished" is information, not an order.
```

⚠ **Never put a smart plug on a 240 V dryer or a gas dryer's power cord unless the plug is rated for it.** Most plugs are for 120 V, 15 A loads. A vibration or door sensor is the safer route there.

**2. Reading care labels.** The tag inside clothes uses symbols. You only need the basics:

| Symbol | Means |
| --- | --- |
| **Washtub** | Machine wash. **Dots** inside are the max temperature: 1 dot = 30 °C (86 °F), 2 dots = 40 °C (104 °F), 3 dots = 50 °C (122 °F), 4 dots = 60 °C (140 °F). A line under the tub means gentle cycle; two lines means very gentle |
| **Washtub with a hand** | Hand wash only (or the machine's hand-wash cycle, if it has one) |
| **Triangle** | Bleach. Empty triangle = any bleach OK. Striped triangle = non-chlorine (oxygen) bleach only |
| **Square with a circle inside** | Tumble dry. Dots = heat level (1 low, 2 medium, 3 high). A filled-in circle = no heat |
| **Square with a line or drooping line** | Dry flat or line dry |
| **Iron** | Ironing, dots = heat |
| **Circle alone** | Dry clean (a pro job) |
| **Any symbol with an X through it** | Don't |

When in doubt: **cold wash, low or no heat dry.** Almost nothing gets ruined that way.

**3. Sorting the lazy-genius way.** Elaborate sorting is mostly a holdover from when dyes ran a lot more. Modern clothes in cold water rarely bleed. A three-bin system covers nearly everything:

| Bin | What | Wash |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Everyday** | Most clothes, any color | Cold, normal cycle |
| **Hot stuff** | Towels, sheets, kitchen towels, underwear and socks if you prefer them hot | Warm or hot, normal or heavy cycle |
| **Animal stuff** | Pet beds, pet blankets, coop clothes, animal towels | Hot, separately (section 6) |

**Delicates** (anything with lace, straps or hooks, like bras) go in a mesh laundry bag in the Everyday load. **Brand-new dark jeans or bright red things** get washed alone or with darks the first couple of times, in case they bleed.

**4. Detergent, load size and temperature.**

- **Use less detergent than you think.** The cap's fill lines are often well above what a normal load needs. Too much soap leaves residue that makes towels stiff, traps smells, and builds up in the machine. Start with the lowest line, or about half the cap for a normal load.
- **HE machines need HE detergent** (look for the "HE" logo). Regular detergent makes too many suds for them.
- ⚠ **Detergent pods:** one per normal load, dropped in the **drum** before the clothes (not the dispenser). Store them high and closed: pods look like candy, and they're toxic if bitten, to people **and** to dogs and cats.
- **Load size:** fill the drum about **three-quarters full, loosely.** Clothes need room to tumble through the water. An overstuffed machine doesn't clean, and it strains the motor.
- **Temperature:**
  - **Cold** for most clothes: saves energy, prevents shrinking and fading.
  - **Warm** for moderately dirty stuff.
  - **Hot** for towels, sheets, kitchen towels, pet bedding, and anything from a sick person or the coop: heat helps kill germs and dust mites.

**5. Stains.** The two rules: **act fast**, and **don't put a stained item in the dryer** until the stain is gone (heat sets stains permanently). Air dry it, check it, and treat again if needed.

| Stain | First move | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Blood, egg, poop, urine, vomit, sweat (proteins)** | **Cold** water, then an enzyme pre-treat or enzymatic cleaner | Hot water cooks protein into the fabric |
| **Grease, oil, butter, makeup** | A drop of **dish soap** rubbed in, sit 10 minutes, then wash warm | Dish soap is a degreaser |
| **Coffee, tea, juice, wine** | Rinse cold from the back of the fabric, then pre-treat | Pushes the stain back out the way it came in |
| **Grass, mud** | Let mud dry and brush it off first, then pre-treat | Wet mud spreads |
| **Red clay dust and dirt** | Shake out outside, then wash | It's fine grit; brushing dry gets most of it |
| **Mystery** | Cold water, dish soap, then enzyme pre-treat | Covers most bases without setting anything |

**6. Pet, coop and animal laundry.**

- **Hair first:** run pet bedding, blankets and hairy clothes through a **10-minute dryer cycle on no heat** before washing. The hair goes to the lint screen instead of clogging the washer. Empty the lint screen after.
- **Wash animal stuff separately, hot**, with an extra rinse.
- **Coop clothes** (and anything worn to clean tanks): shake out **outside**, wash separately and hot. These can carry Salmonella (Week 10).
- **Urine-soaked bedding:** rinse cold first, soak with enzymatic cleaner per its label, then wash.
- **Animal-only towels** stay animal-only.
- **After washing a load of pet bedding**, run a short rinse cycle with the drum empty, or wipe out the drum, to get stray hair out before the next people load.

**7. Drying, and Folding is Optional.**

- **Clean the lint screen every load** (⚠ the fire one, Week 9).
- **Shake things out** before they go in the dryer: fewer wrinkles, faster drying.
- **Don't over-dry.** It wastes energy, shrinks cotton, and wears fabric out. Most loads are done in 40–60 minutes on medium.
- **The sun is a free dryer.** On a hot, dry day a line or rack outside dries most loads in an hour or two. Bring things in before wind, dust or rain, and turn darks inside-out (the sun fades them).
- **Dryer sheets vs dryer balls:** dryer sheets leave a waxy film on the lint screen (Week 9) and add fragrance that cats and reptiles don't need. **Wool dryer balls** soften, cut drying time a bit, and last for years.

**No-fold systems** (pick whichever fits your brain):

- **Hang straight from the dryer:** shirts, pants and dresses go on hangers the moment they come out. No wrinkles, no folding, and they're already "put away" when you hang them.
- **The bin system:** one open bin per category (underwear, socks, pajamas, workout clothes, towels). Toss, don't fold. Done.
- **The clean basket:** clean clothes live in a basket, and you get dressed out of it. This is a complete, valid system. The only rule: keep the clean basket and dirty hamper visibly different (different color or shape) so they never get mixed up.
- **The "worn once" hook:** a hook or chair that's officially for clothes that aren't dirty but aren't fresh. It stops the floor from becoming that place.

**8. How often to wash what.**

| Item | How often | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Underwear, socks, sports gear | After each wear | Sweat and bacteria |
| T-shirts, tops | After 1–2 wears | Sweat and body oil, especially in hot weather |
| Jeans, pants | After 3–5 wears, or when dirty | Washing less keeps them lasting longer |
| Pajamas | After 3–4 wears | Skin cells and body oil |
| Bath towels | After 3–4 uses (hang to dry between uses) | Damp fabric grows bacteria and mildew |
| Kitchen towels and dish cloths | Every 1–2 days | They touch food and wet hands all day |
| Sheets and pillowcases | Weekly (every 3–4 days if pets share the bed or it's a sweaty summer) | Skin cells, sweat, dust mites, pet dander |
| Blankets and throws | Monthly (pet blankets weekly to every 2 weeks) | Dust and hair |
| Pillows | Every 6 months (check the label) | Sweat and dust mites build up inside |
| Mattress protector | Monthly | It catches everything so the mattress doesn't |
| Pet beds and covers | Weekly to every 2 weeks | Hair, dander, saliva, flea eggs |

**Smelly towels fix** (musty even after washing): wash hot with about **250 mL white vinegar and no detergent**, then wash again normally with a **small** amount of detergent. Don't put the vinegar and detergent in the same cycle; they work against each other. The usual culprit is too much detergent or towels left damp, so fix that too.

**The three sizes:**

- **Crumb:** move whatever's in the washer to the dryer. That's the most important transfer in the whole loop.
- **Standard:** one load through all five stages, with "put away" meaning hung up, binned, or in the clean basket.
- **Great Scott:** every hamper empty, all the bedding (yours and the animals'), the washer gasket wiped and a cleaning cycle run.

- **ENERGY STAR:** [Clothes Washers](https://www.energystar.gov/products/clothes_washers) (cold-water and efficiency tips)
- **America's Poison Centers:** [poisoncenters.org](https://poisoncenters.org/) (detergent pod safety)

### Week 8: Bedrooms, the Recharge Bay

- What the bedroom is for, and why it's worth protecting
- The bed: airing, making (optional), and changing sheets without a wrestling match
- Animals in the bed
- Mattress and pillow care
- The clutter zones: nightstand, floor, closet
- Dust, and the under-bed scorpion check
- Building a sensory sanctuary
- **Bench Task menu:** clear the nightstand down to what you actually use at night; put a small trash can and a hamper where things land; change the sheets with the corner trick; vacuum under the bed with a flashlight.

#### Shop Manual: read this first

*"Every inventor needs a place to power down, apprentice. Even me, and I'm mostly caffeine and spite. The bedroom is the charging dock for your brain. It doesn't have to be pretty. It has to be calm, clean enough to breathe in, and set up so you can find your socks in the morning without an archaeological dig."* — Twig

**1. What the bedroom is for.** Sleep, quiet and recovery. For AuDHD brains, the bedroom is often the one place where sensory input can be turned all the way down, so a little upkeep here pays off more than anywhere else. It's also where a lot of dust lives: bedrooms collect skin cells, fabric fibers, pet dander and dust mites, which can mean stuffy noses and worse sleep.

**2. The bed.**

| Task | How often | Why | If skipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pull the covers back** in the morning to air the bed | Daily, optional | Lets the night's moisture out; dust mites like it damp | Nothing dramatic; a slightly mustier bed |
| Make the bed | **Optional** | Some brains find a made bed calming; others don't care. Both are fine | Nothing. Really |
| Change and wash sheets and pillowcases | Weekly; every 3–4 days if pets sleep there | Skin cells, sweat, body oil, dust mites, pet dander | Smell, itchiness, allergies |
| Wash the mattress protector | Monthly | It catches sweat, spills and accidents | They reach the mattress instead |
| Wash pillows (check the label) and blankets | Pillows every 6 months; blankets monthly | Sweat soaks in over time | Yellowed, smelly pillows |

**Changing sheets without the wrestling match:**

1. Strip everything. Pillowcases off, sheets into the hamper (or straight into the washer).
2. **Fitted sheet:** find the corner seams (the tag is usually on a short-side corner). Put on **one corner**, then the **diagonally opposite corner**, then the other two. Going diagonal stops the first corner from popping off while you do the last one.
3. **Top sheet:** optional. Plenty of people skip it and just use a duvet cover.
4. **Duvet cover, the "burrito" method:** turn the cover inside out and lay it flat on the bed, opening at the foot. Lay the duvet on top of it. Starting at the head end, roll both together toward the foot like a burrito. Reach into the opening, flip it around the roll from the opening end, and close it up. Unroll toward the head. It comes out right side out with the duvet inside. (Search "duvet burrito method" for a 1-minute video; it's easier to see than to read.)
5. Pillowcases on.

**Done looks like:** fresh sheets on, dirty sheets in the washer (not on the floor).

**The two-set trick:** keep **two sets of sheets per bed**: one on the bed, one clean in the closet. Store each clean set folded **inside one of its own pillowcases**, so the whole set is one grab and nothing goes missing.

**3. Animals in the bed.** If the dogs or cats sleep on the bed (Twig does not judge; Twig sleeps in a drawer):

- **Wash sheets every 3–4 days to weekly**, as hot as the label allows.
- **A washable throw or blanket on top** where they sleep catches most of the hair and paw dirt. Wash it weekly, and the sheets underneath stay cleaner longer.
- A **waterproof mattress protector** is essential with pets (accidents, hairballs, vomit).
- Keep **flea prevention current** (Week 10); fleas love bedding.

**4. Mattress and pillows.**

- **Mattress protector:** waterproof, washable, always on. It's the cheapest way to keep a mattress lasting.
- **Vacuum the mattress** every 3–6 months with the upholstery tool. For a refresh, sprinkle baking soda, leave it an hour or more, then vacuum it up.
- **Rotate the mattress** head-to-foot every 3–6 months (and flip it only if it's a two-sided type) so it wears evenly.
- **Spills and accidents:** blot, then enzymatic cleaner for urine or vomit (Week 10), and dry fully with a fan before remaking the bed.
- **Pillow test:** fold the pillow in half. If it doesn't spring back open, it's time for a new one (often every 1–2 years).

**5. The clutter zones.**

| Zone | What happens | Engineering fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nightstand** | Cups, wrappers, chargers, books, mystery stuff | A **small trash can** right next to it; one cup allowed (Week 6); a tray for what you actually use at night (glasses, phone, lip balm); everything else goes |
| **The floor** | Clothes, shoes, bags | A **hamper where clothes actually land** (not where they "should" land), a "worn once" hook (Week 7), a shoe spot by the door |
| **The chair** | Becomes a clothing mountain | Make it official: that's the worn-once spot. Clear it when it's full, not on a schedule |
| **The closet** | A drop zone | Hooks and open bins instead of hangers and drawers where possible; a **donate bag** that lives in the closet so outgrown or unloved clothes have an exit |

**Bedroom Five Things:** trash, dishes, laundry, things with a place, things without a place (Week 2). The bedroom usually goes: trash and cups in 5 minutes, then the laundry pile, and it already looks very different.

**Closet seasonal swap:** bin the off-season clothes in a labeled tote on a high shelf, and swap again when the weather turns. Less in the closet means it's easier to see what you have.

**6. Dust, and the under-bed check.**

| Task | How often | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dust nightstands, dresser and shelves (top to bottom) | Weekly | Bedroom dust is skin cells, fibers and dander; you breathe it for 8 hours |
| Vacuum the floor | Weekly; more with pets | Same, plus hair |
| Vacuum **under the bed** with a flashlight | Monthly | Dust bunnies, lost things, and a favorite hiding spot for crickets and **scorpions** |
| Ceiling fan blades | Monthly (Week 4) | A dusty fan above the bed sprinkles you all night |
| Wash or dust curtains and blinds | Every 3–6 months | Dust and pet hair |

**Scorpion-smart bedroom habits:** pull the bed a few centimeters away from the wall, keep bedding from touching the floor, shake out shoes and clothes from the floor before putting them on, and do the monthly blacklight sweep along the baseboards (Week 10).

**7. The sensory sanctuary.** Optional upgrades that make the room work better for an AuDHD nervous system:

- **Light:** blackout curtains or a sleep mask; warm, dimmable lamps instead of an overhead light; smart bulbs that dim on a schedule.
- **Sound:** a fan or white-noise machine; door draft stoppers cut hallway noise.
- **Temperature:** many people sleep best in a cool room. A ceiling fan on counterclockwise (Week 4) and breathable cotton or linen sheets help a lot in hot summers.
- **Texture:** pick sheets by feel (cotton percale is crisp and cool, sateen is smooth, jersey is t-shirt soft, linen is textured and very breathable). Your skin gets the final vote.
- **Weighted blankets:** check the label before washing. Many have a removable cover that washes normally while the heavy insert gets washed rarely (or at a laundromat's big machine). Heavy wet blankets can strain a home washer.
- **Less stuff in sight:** closed bins or a curtain over open shelves cut visual noise at bedtime.

**The three sizes:**

- **Crumb:** take every cup out of the bedroom. Or throw one handful of trash away.
- **Standard:** cups and trash out, clothes into the hamper or onto the worn-once hook, covers pulled back.
- **Great Scott:** sheets changed, mattress vacuumed and rotated, under the bed vacuumed with a scorpion check, nightstand cleared to just the night tray, and a donate bag filled.

- **American Lung Association:** [Dust Mites](https://www.lung.org/clean-air/indoor-air/indoor-air-pollutants/dust-mites)

**1. ⚠ Fire and safety first.** These are the ones the house can't recover from if skipped.

| Task | How often | Why | If skipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Clean the dryer lint screen** | **Every load** | Lint is extremely flammable, and a clogged screen makes the dryer run hot. Failure to clean is the leading cause of home dryer fires, per the NFPA | Fire risk; clothes take forever to dry |
| Wash the lint screen with soap and a soft brush | Every few months | Dryer sheets leave an invisible waxy film that blocks airflow even when the screen looks clean. Test: run water over it; if water pools, it needs washing | Same as above, sneakier |
| **Clean the dryer vent duct** (the hose behind the dryer and the outside vent) | At least yearly; sooner if drying takes longer than it used to | Lint builds up inside the duct where you can't see it | Fire risk |
| **Test smoke alarms** (press and hold the test button) | **Monthly** | Alarms fail silently; the button tests the alarm and the battery | No warning in a fire |
| Replace smoke alarm batteries (if replaceable) | Yearly, or right away when it chirps | Batteries die | The 3 a.m. chirp, or no alarm |
| **Replace smoke alarm units** | **10 years from the date on the back** | Sensors wear out | An alarm that "works" when tested but doesn't detect smoke well |
| Test CO alarms (if the house has gas appliances, a fireplace or an attached garage) | Monthly; replace per the date on the unit (often 7–10 years) | Carbon monoxide has no smell | Poisoning with no warning |
| Check the fire extinguisher gauge | Monthly | The needle should be in the green | Empty extinguisher in an emergency |
| Press TEST then RESET on GFCI outlets (bathroom, kitchen, garage, outdoor) | Monthly | GFCIs prevent electrocution near water; they can fail | No protection near water |

```lab
**The monthly safety lap** (about 10 minutes, a good first-of-the-month habit): press every smoke and CO alarm test button (earplugs help; warn the dogs), glance at the extinguisher gauge, press TEST/RESET on each GFCI, and pull the dryer lint screen to check for film. Log it. Done.
```

One more from the NFPA's dryer tips: **don't leave the dryer running when you leave the house or go to bed.** If something goes wrong, you want to be there to smell it.

**2. The HVAC (air conditioner) filter.** With dust, pets and the heating or AC running much of the year, this is **the** maintenance item.

- **Where it is:** usually behind a big return grille in a wall or ceiling (the one that *pulls* air in, not the ones that blow), or in a slot at the air handler. Some houses have more than one.
- **What size:** printed on the edge of the old filter (like 20×25×1 in). Write it in the House Log and buy several at once.
- **How often:** **check monthly; change when it looks gray or clogged.** In a house with several pets or a dusty climate, expect **every 30–60 days** for a standard 2.5 cm (1 in) filter; thicker media filters last longer. Always check after a dust storm or wildfire smoke.
- **Why:** a clogged filter starves the AC of air. It works harder, uses more electricity, can freeze up or overheat, and stops cleaning the air.
- **How:** AC off at the thermostat. Pull the old filter out. Note the **arrow on the frame**: it points **toward the air handler / into the duct** (the way the air flows). Slide the new one in, arrow the same way. AC back on. Log it.
- **Plus:** a yearly professional AC tune-up in **spring, before the heat**, catches failing parts before they fail at 46 °C (115 °F).

**3. The kitchen machines.**

| Machine | Task | How often | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fridge** | Vacuum the condenser coils (behind the bottom kick plate or on the back; **unplug first**) | Every 6–12 months; every 3–6 with shedding pets | Dusty coils make the compressor run hot and long; it costs electricity and shortens its life |
| | Wipe the door gasket (rubber seal) with warm soapy water | Monthly | Crumbs and sticky gunk stop the seal from closing tight |
| | Replace the water/ice filter (if it has one) | Per the manual, often ~6 months | Old filters grow bacteria and slow the flow |
| | Check temperatures: fridge 4 °C (40 °F) or below, freezer −18 °C (0 °F) | Monthly, with a cheap fridge thermometer | Food safety |
| **Dishwasher** | Pull and rinse the filter at the bottom (twist out under the lower spray arm) | Monthly | Food bits collect there; a clogged filter means gritty dishes and a smell |
| | Run an empty hot cycle with dishwasher cleaner or a cup of white vinegar on the top rack | Monthly with hard water | Dissolves mineral film and grease |
| | Check spray arm holes for clogs (poke out with a toothpick) | Every few months | Blocked holes mean dirty dishes |
| **Microwave** | Steam clean (Week 11 lemon method) and wipe | Weekly | Splatter bakes on |
| **Range hood** | Degrease the metal filter: soak in very hot water with dish soap and a few spoons of baking soda, 15 min, scrub, rinse, dry | Monthly if you cook a lot | Grease buildup is a fire risk and the fan stops pulling smoke |
| **Oven** | Wipe spills once cool; deep clean with self-clean cycle or baking soda paste | Spills: soon. Deep: every 3–6 months | Burnt spills smoke and smell |
| **Garbage disposal** | Grind a handful of ice cubes, then flush with cold water; citrus peels for freshness (Week 11) | Monthly | Ice knocks gunk off the blades; cold water keeps grease solid so it goes through |

⚠ **Self-clean ovens and birds:** self-clean cycles run extremely hot and can release fumes that are dangerous to birds. Ventilate well, keep any indoor birds far away (backyard birds live outside, which helps). Many people skip self-clean and use the baking soda method instead: paste of baking soda and water on the cool oven interior, sit overnight, wipe out, then spray a little vinegar to foam off the residue, and wipe again.

**Garbage disposal never-list:** grease or oil (it solidifies in the pipe), fibrous stuff (celery, corn husks, artichoke), potato peels in bulk (they make paste), coffee grounds in bulk, eggshells in bulk, bones, pits. **Never put your hand in it.** If it's jammed: switch off at the wall, then use the hex key in the bottom socket or tongs.

| **Washer (any)** | Run a cleaning cycle (washer cleaner, or per the manual) | Monthly | Detergent and mineral buildup, plus pet hair |

**Pet hair and laundry:** run pet bedding and blankets through a 10-minute **dryer cycle on no heat** before washing. It loosens hair into the lint screen instead of your washer. Empty the screen after.

**5. Small appliances.**

| Thing | Task | How often | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Kettle, coffee maker, espresso machine** | Descale: run a cycle with half white vinegar, half water (or citric acid), then 2–3 cycles of plain water | Monthly with hard water (manual may say differently) | Hard water scale slows heating and makes coffee taste flat |
| **Toaster / toaster oven** | Empty the crumb tray (**unplugged**) | Weekly-ish | Crumbs burn and can catch fire |
| **Vacuum** | Empty bin, cut hair off the brush roll, wash/replace filter | Bin each use; brush roll monthly; filter per manual | Lost suction |
| **Air purifiers** | Replace filters per the manual | Usually 6–12 months | A dead filter does nothing |
| **Humidifier** (if used in winter) | Empty, dry and clean | Every few days in use | They grow bacteria and mold fast |

**6. The water heater and the softener.**

- **Water heater (tank type):** hard water drops **sediment** (minerals) to the bottom of the tank. It insulates the water from the heater, makes popping or rumbling sounds, and shortens the tank's life. **Flush it yearly; every 6 months is common advice with very hard water.** The **anode rod** inside (a sacrificial metal rod that corrodes so the tank doesn't) wears out in a few years; check it every 2–3 years.
- ⚠ Flushing involves scalding water, gas or high-voltage power, and valves. For the first time, either **watch a video for your exact model**, have someone experienced show you, or have a plumber do it while you watch. After that, it's a 30–60 minute job.
- **Water softener (if you have one):** check the salt level monthly and keep it topped up (it should be at least a third full); break up any hard salt "bridge" crust with a broom handle.
- **Tankless water heater:** needs a descaling flush with a pump kit, often yearly in hard water areas. Check the manual.

*"Every one of your animals is a delight, apprentice, and every one of them is also a little walking germ shuttle. That's not a reason to love them less; it's a reason to have a system. Wash your hands, keep their stuff out of the kitchen, and use the right cleaner for each kind of mess. That's 90% of it. The other 10% is hair. So much hair."* — Twig

**1. ⚠ The germ map.** Animals can look perfectly healthy and still carry germs that make people sick. This isn't about the animals being dirty; it's normal biology. Know where the risk is:

| Resident | Main risk to people | Where it lives | Key rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Reptiles (tortoises, turtles, lizards, snakes)** | **Salmonella**, very commonly carried by healthy reptiles | Droppings, skin, shell, tank water, substrate, everything in the habitat | **Never** clean habitats, dishes or decor in the **kitchen sink**, or anywhere food is made |
| **Backyard chickens and ducks** | **Salmonella** and other gut bacteria | Droppings, feathers, feet, coop dust, eggs, feeders and waterers | **Poultry and their gear stay outside**; coop shoes stay outside |
| **Cats** | **Toxoplasma** (in feces), bacteria from scratches | Litter box | **Scoop daily** (see below); wash hands |
| **Dogs** | Gut bacteria and parasites in feces; fleas | Yard, paws, bedding | Pick up poop; flea prevention; wash hands |

**The handwashing rule** (this one carries the most weight): **wash with soap and running water for 20 seconds** after touching any animal, its food, its water, its habitat or its poop, and before touching food or your face. Keep soap and a towel at the back door and by the reptile room so it's always one step away.

**2. Zones and dedicated gear.** Separate the animal stuff from the people stuff so germs have no road to the kitchen.

- **An animal-gear bucket, brush and sponges**, marked (a strip of colored tape works), used **only** for animal habitats and dishes. Never used in the kitchen.
- **Where to wash reptile gear:** outside with a hose, or a laundry sink, or a bathtub that you **clean and disinfect right afterward** (Week 1 bleach mix, rinse). Never the kitchen sink or a sink used for food.
- **Where to wash poultry gear:** **outside.** Feeders, waterers and coop tools never come in the house.
- **Coop shoes:** a dedicated pair (rubber boots or slides) that live by the coop or at the back door, **outside**.
- **Pet food bowls:** wash daily in hot soapy water, separately from people dishes, or run them in the dishwasher on their own load. (Dog and cat bowls grow a slimy film called biofilm within a day.)
- **Animal-only towels**, kept separate from people towels.

**3. Hair, dander and smell.**

| Task | How often | Why | If skipped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vacuum busy floors and pet hangout spots | 2–3× a week; daily in shedding season | Hair and dander build fast and feed dust mites | Hair tumbleweeds; allergies |
| Brush dogs and cats (outside if possible) | A few times a week in shedding season | Every hair in the brush is a hair not on the couch | More vacuuming |
| Wash pet beds, blankets and couch covers | Weekly to every 2 weeks | Hair, dander, saliva, dirt, and sometimes fleas | Smell; flea eggs hatch in bedding |
| Lint-roll or rubber-glove the couch | Weekly | Fabric holds hair | Hair on everything you wear |
| HVAC filter check | Monthly (Week 9) | Hair and dander load filters fast | AC strain; dusty air |

**About smell:** a house with animals smells like animals when something is **actively** dirty: a litter box, a bed, an old urine spot, a tank, or a filter. Air fresheners and scented plug-ins just cover it (and many are bad for cats and reptiles). **Find the source** and clean it. Baking soda sprinkled on carpet for 30 minutes before vacuuming absorbs some odor and is pet-safe once vacuumed up.

**4. Urine, vomit and poop: the enzymatic method.** Pet urine soaks deep and contains compounds that regular cleaners don't break down. When an animal can still smell its old spot, it often goes there again. **Enzymatic cleaners** (pet stores carry them) contain enzymes that actually digest the urine.

1. **Blot up** as much as possible with paper towels. Press; don't rub (rubbing spreads it). For solids, scoop first.
2. **Do not use ammonia cleaners** (urine contains ammonia; to the animal it smells like "go here") and **never bleach** (bleach plus urine's ammonia makes chloramine gas). **Don't steam clean** or use hot water on urine: heat can set the stain and smell.
3. **Saturate** the spot with enzymatic cleaner, enough to reach as deep as the urine went (on carpet, that's into the pad).
4. **Leave it wet** for the time on the label (often 10–15 minutes or more). Some products say to cover with a towel and let it air dry for a day. **The enzymes only work while wet.**
5. Blot and let it **air dry fully.** Don't use another cleaner on top; other chemicals kill the enzymes.
6. **Done looks like:** no smell with your nose right at the spot after it's dry, and the animal doesn't go back.

**Finding old spots:** a **UV blacklight flashlight** in a dark room makes dried urine glow. Bonus: **scorpions glow bright blue-green under UV**, so in scorpion country a blacklight sweep doubles as a scorpion hunt. Mark spots with sticky notes, then treat them all.

```tip
**Repeat offenders aren't being spiteful.** A cat peeing outside the box is often a medical problem (urinary issues are common and can be an emergency in male cats) or a litter box problem (dirty, wrong spot, not enough boxes). Talk to the vet.
```

**5. Litter boxes.**

| Task | How often | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scoop | **At least daily** | Cats avoid dirty boxes. Also: the Toxoplasma parasite shed in cat feces takes 1–5 days to become infectious, so daily scooping removes it before it can be |
| Top up litter | As needed (keep it about 5–8 cm (2–3 in) deep) | Clumps hold together better in deeper litter |
| Full empty, wash and refill | Every 2–4 weeks for clumping litter; weekly for non-clumping | Urine soaks into the plastic and the old litter |
| Replace the box itself | About yearly | Scratches in old plastic hold odor |

**Washing the box:** dump the litter, wash with hot water and **unscented** dish soap, rinse, dry fully. Skip strong-smelling cleaners (cats may refuse the box) and **never bleach on a box with urine residue** unless it's been washed and rinsed with soap and water first. Wear gloves. People who are pregnant or have weakened immune systems are advised to have someone else do litter if possible, or wear gloves and wash hands well.

**Rule of thumb for how many boxes:** one per cat, plus one, spread across the house.

**6. The reptiles' habitats.** Each species has its own setup (follow its care sheet or your vet); this is just the cleaning side.

| Task | How often | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spot-clean: remove poop, urates, uneaten food and soiled substrate | **Daily** | Waste grows bacteria fast in a warm, humid tank |
| Wash water and food dishes | Daily | Tortoises often poop in their water |
| Partial substrate change and wipe walls | Every 1–2 weeks (depends on substrate and setup) | Buildup of waste and bacteria |
| **Full deep clean** of the tank and all decor | About monthly, or per the care card | Resets the habitat |

**The tank deep clean:**

1. Move the tortoise to a **safe temporary tub** with a hide and the right warmth. Keep it short; reptiles need their heat.
2. Take everything out. Dump the old substrate (bag it to the trash, or the compost only if it's plant-based and the compost doesn't feed the food garden).
3. **Take decor, dishes and hides outside** (or to a bathtub you'll disinfect after). Scrub with hot soapy water and the animal-only brush.
4. **Clean** the empty tank with hot soapy water; scrape any stuck waste.
5. **Disinfect:** the Week 1 bleach mix (about 20 mL per 1 L, or per the label), or a reptile-safe veterinary disinfectant per its label. Leave wet for the label's contact time.
6. **Rinse thoroughly** with plain water, several times, until there's no smell of bleach at all.
7. **Air dry completely**, ideally in the sun (UV helps too). No lingering fumes before the animal goes back in.
8. Fresh substrate, decor back, check the temperature and humidity are where they belong, then return the animal.
9. **Wash your hands.** Then disinfect the bathtub or sink you used.

**Never** use phenol cleaners, pine oil, essential oils or scented products in any reptile habitat.

**Aquariums (turtles, fish):** the water itself is part of the habitat and **is not cleaned with soap or disinfectant while the animal lives in it** (any residue can harm it). Partial water changes and filter care follow its care sheet. The house-side rules: carry tank water in the animal-only bucket, **dump it in the toilet, the bathtub (then disinfect it), or outside away from the food garden**, never in the kitchen sink. Aquarium gloves and a towel live by the tank.

**7. Outdoor bird gear** (if you keep backyard chickens or ducks). House-side rules from CDC:

- Feeders, waterers, buckets and tools are **cleaned outside**, never in the house.
- **Coop shoes stay outside.**
- **Eggs:** collect often; brush off dirt with a dry cloth or fine sandpaper. If they need washing, use water **warmer** than the egg (cold water can pull germs into the shell). Refrigerate.
- **Wash hands** after collecting eggs and handling birds or their gear.

**8. Pest patrol.** Animals, food, water and warm weather attract pests. Cleaning is the first line of defense.

- **Fleas:** monthly vet-recommended prevention for the dogs and cats is the real fix. If fleas show up anyway: vacuum daily (floors, couches, pet beds; empty the vacuum outside right away), wash all bedding hot, and treat the animals. Flea eggs hatch in bedding and carpet, which is why the cleaning matters. **Many dog flea products are toxic to cats**; never use a dog product on a cat.
- **Crickets and roaches:** clean up food crumbs, pet food spills and standing water; store pet food in sealed bins.
- **Scorpions** (in areas that have them, such as the US Southwest): they hunt crickets and roaches, so fewer bugs means fewer scorpions. Reduce clutter (especially piles on floors and in closets), shake out shoes and towels, pull beds a little away from walls, check baseboard gaps and door sweeps, and do monthly blacklight sweeps along walls at night. Sticky traps along walls catch them. A sting on a child or anyone with severe symptoms is a call to Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) or 911.

**9. The Full House Deep Clean, in modules.** A whole-house deep clean in one day is a recipe for burnout. Do it as **modules**: each one stands alone, takes 30–90 minutes, and can happen on any day, in any order. Tick them off over a week or a month.

| Module | What's in it | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Bedding day** | Every pet bed, blanket, couch cover and throw: no-heat dryer tumble to loosen hair, then hot wash; human sheets too | Mostly waiting |
| **B. The blacklight sweep** | Lights off, UV flashlight, mark every urine spot (and evict any scorpions); enzymatic treatment on all of them | 45 min + dwell time |
| **C. High dust** | Fans, vents, returns, light fixtures, tops of things, cobwebs; then change the HVAC filter | 45 min |
| **D. Floors, deep** | Move the furniture, vacuum under and behind everything, scrub grout, mop everything; carpet extractor on pet zones | 90 min |
| **E. Litter reset** | Every box emptied, washed, dried and refilled; floor and wall around each box scrubbed | 45 min |
| **F. Reptile tank reset** | Full deep clean of one tank (one per session is plenty); one per module run | 60 min per tank |
| **G. Kitchen deep** | Fridge out and wiped, coils vacuumed, oven, range hood filter, cabinet fronts, inside the microwave, disposal | 90 min |
| **H. Bathroom deep** | Grout scrub, showerhead descale, drain hair, curtain liner wash, caulk check | 60 min |
| **I. Outside the door** | Doormats shaken and washed, back door handwash station restocked, coop shoes cleaned, outdoor animal gear scrubbed outside | 45 min |

#### Shop Manual: read this first

*"A gnome never buys what he can grow, apprentice! The garden and the fruit bowl give you citrus, rosemary, and if you plant the right vine, actual sponges. Breakfast gives you eggshells. Free cleaning supplies, compost when they're used up, and the whole kitchen smells like a lemon grove instead of a chemistry lab. Just know their limits: lemons don't disinfect, and cats don't love citrus."* — Twig

**1. What the grove can give you.**

| Source | Cleaning use | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Citrus peels** (lemon, orange, grapefruit, any citrus from the grove) | Infused vinegar; fresh peel rubs; disposal freshener | Degreasing, mineral spots, smell |
| **Lemons** (juice and halves) | Mild acid scrub with salt; microwave steam | Hard water spots, cutting boards, copper and brass tarnish, microwave grime |
| **Rosemary** (and sage) | Infused vinegar for scent | Floors and counters where you'd rather not use citrus |
| **Eggshells** (from breakfast or the flock) | Dried and ground into a scrubbing powder | Stuck-on food in pots, cast iron, mugs with tea stains |
| **Luffa gourd** (a summer vine you can grow) | Natural, compostable scrub sponges | Dishes, body, scrubbing |
| **Compost** | Where all of the above go when used up | Closing the loop |

**2. ⚠ The honest limits.**

- **Homegrown cleaners clean; they don't disinfect.** Vinegar and citrus acid kill some germs, but not to disinfectant standards. For raw meat spills, toilets and animal habitats, use a real disinfectant (Week 1).
- **Acids still etch natural stone** and can dull wood finishes and some metal finishes. Citrus vinegar follows all the vinegar rules.
- **Never combine any of these with bleach.** Citrus vinegar is an acid. Acid plus bleach is chlorine gas.
- **Cats and citrus:** concentrated citrus **oils** are toxic to cats. A diluted peel vinegar on a counter that's then wiped and dried is a far lower exposure, but keep cats off until it's dry, and **don't use citrus cleaners on floors cats walk and lick, cat beds, litter areas or food bowls.** For those areas, use rosemary vinegar or plain soap and water. (Rosemary is listed as non-toxic to cats and dogs by the ASPCA; mint and lavender are not, so they're left out here.)
- **Reptile habitats get none of these.** Unscented soap and water, then proper disinfectant, rinsed and dried (Week 10).

**3. Recipe: citrus peel vinegar** (the multipurpose spray).

1. Pack a clean glass jar with citrus peels (lemon, orange, grapefruit, mixed is fine). Scrape off most of the white pith if you have the patience; it's fine if you don't. Collect peels in a bag in the freezer until you have a jarful.
2. Cover completely with **white vinegar**. Peels poking out of the vinegar can mold; weigh them down with a small jar or a fermentation weight.
3. Lid on, label it with the **date**, and put it somewhere dark for **2 weeks**. Shake it whenever you remember (or don't).
4. Strain out the peels (they go to the compost; **not** to the pets).
5. **Mix 1 part citrus vinegar to 1 part water** in a labeled spray bottle.

**Use it on:** glass, mirrors, sinks and faucets (mineral spots), stovetop grease, sealed laminate or tile counters, trash cans, inside the fridge. **Not on:** natural stone, hardwood floors, cast iron, electronics screens, or anywhere bleach was just used (rinse first).

**4. Recipe: rosemary vinegar** (the cat-friendlier version). Same method as the citrus: pack a jar with rosemary sprigs from the grove, cover with white vinegar, 1–2 weeks, strain, dilute 1:1. Smells like an herb garden instead of a salad. Use it the same places, plus floors (tile, vinyl; not stone or wood) in a mop bucket: about **250 mL rosemary vinegar in 4 L warm water.**

**5. Lemon tricks.**

- **Microwave steam clean:** a microwave-safe bowl with about **250 mL water** and a few lemon slices or lemon juice. **Put a wooden spoon or chopstick in the bowl** (it prevents superheated water from suddenly boiling over when you move it). Microwave on high for 3 minutes, then leave the door **shut** for 5 more minutes. Open carefully (steam is hot), and the grime wipes right off.
- **Lemon and salt scrub:** cut a lemon in half, dip the cut side in coarse salt, and scrub. Good on wood cutting boards (deodorizes; rinse and dry after), copper and brass tarnish, and hard water spots on faucets. Rinse and dry.
- **Disposal freshener:** a few small pieces of citrus peel with a handful of ice, cold water running. Not big pieces, and not a lot at once.
- **Fridge deodorizer:** half a lemon on a saucer, or the classic: an open box of baking soda.

**6. Eggshell scrub** (from breakfast or the flock).

1. Rinse shells and let them dry.
2. **Bake them dry** in the oven at **about 120 °C (250 °F) for 10–15 minutes.** This dries them completely and deals with any leftover egg (and Salmonella).
3. Crush them coarsely (a bag and a rolling pin, or a mortar and pestle). Store in a labeled jar.
4. **Use:** sprinkle a spoonful into a pot or cast iron pan with a drop of dish soap and a little water, scrub with a brush or cloth. They're a gentle abrasive, like a free version of a scouring powder. Great on tea-stained mugs too.
5. Rinse the shells down the drain sparingly (**not the disposal in bulk**) or, better, into the compost.

**7. Grow your own sponges: luffa.** The **luffa** (loofah) is a gourd vine that loves hot weather, so it suits a sunny garden trellis in the warm season. Young gourds are edible like a summer squash. Left on the vine until the skin turns brown, dry and light, the inside becomes the fibrous sponge you know.

1. **Plant** after the last frost when the soil is warm (spring). Give it a strong trellis; the vines get long.
2. **Harvest** when the skin is brown and dry and the gourd feels light and rattles.
3. **Peel** off the dry skin (soaking in water for a bit makes it easier). Shake out the seeds; save some for next year.
4. **Rinse** and let it dry in the sun.
5. **Cut** into rounds or slabs. Use as dish scrubbers, body sponges, or scrub pads.
6. When it's worn out, **compost it.**

```tip
**Garden tie-in:** give luffa its own trellis so it doesn't swallow its neighbors. And keep young vines out of reach of any tortoises or chickens; they'll happily "prune" anything at their height.
```

## Rescue Mode: When It's All Too Much

*"Some days the house wins, apprentice. Some weeks, too. That's not a failure; it's weather. When the whole place looks like a contraption exploded, we don't 'clean the house.' We do one tiny thing, then decide if there's another one. That's the whole method."* — Twig

**First, the reframe.** A messy house isn't a verdict on you. It's a pile of tasks that haven't happened yet, usually because life, energy, executive function or health got in the way. The house doesn't care *why*. It just needs a few things moved.

**The Rescue order** (stop after any step; each one makes the house work a little better):

1. **Safety first.** Anything blocking an exit or a walkway, anything on the stove, anything leaking, any food rotting? Handle that one thing.
2. **Animals' basics.** Water, food, and litter scooped. They can't do it themselves.
3. **Trash.** One bag. Walk through and pick up only trash. (Five Things, step 1.)
4. **Dishes.** Collect them in one spot. Run the dishwasher, or soak them in hot soapy water and walk away (the soak does the work).
5. **Laundry.** Gather it into one pile or basket. Start one load. (You don't have to fold anything. Clean clothes in a basket are a complete system.)
6. **One surface.** Clear the kitchen counter, or your bedside table, or the couch. Just one.
7. **Check in.** How's the battery? Stopping here is a win.

**Tools for the hard days:**

- **Paper plates and cups** are a legitimate tool. They're not cheating; they're a load reduction.
- **A "clean with me" video or a friend on the phone** for body doubling.
- **The 5-song rule:** put on five songs, do whatever you feel like doing until they end, then stop.
- **Hire help** if you can: a cleaning service once, or once a month, for the deep stuff. Many people with ADHD and autism use this as a support, the same as glasses or a calendar.
- **Lower the bar** for a while. Daily list down to: animals fed and watered, litter scooped, food trash out. That's enough to keep everyone safe.

```tip
**The house will forgive you.** Almost everything in this workshop can wait a week or a month without real harm. The handful that can't (lint screen, never-mix, the animals' basics, rotting food) are all on the first two lines of Rescue Mode. Everything else is patient.
```

## Sleep Help for AuDHD and PDA Brains

*"Even the greatest inventor has to power down eventually, apprentice. Trouble is, some of our brains treat 11 p.m. like the opening bell of the idea factory. That's not a character flaw; it's wiring. This page is a parts bin of things that help, ones you can try, swap or ignore. No bedtime police. I'm a gnome, not a hall monitor. Heh."* — Twig

**First, the reframe.** Sleep trouble is common in AuDHD brains, and it isn't a willpower problem. Research reviews find that trouble falling and staying asleep, and a **delayed sleep-wake phase** (a body clock that runs late), are very common in autistic people and people with ADHD, and in people who are both. Many people with ADHD in particular have a body clock that pushes sleep later, plus racing thoughts and restlessness that make winding down harder. So the goal isn't "go to bed on time like a good person." It's to **work with the clock you've got**, and to get real help if it's wrecking your days.

### Why it's harder for us (and what each one needs)

| Snag | What's going on | What tends to help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Late body clock** | The brain releases its sleep signal later than average, so "bedtime" feels like mid-afternoon | Bright light in the morning, dim light at night, a steady **wake** time (more below) |
| **Racing brain** | Ideas, replays, to-do lists and "what if" loops arrive the moment it's quiet | A brain-dump notebook by the bed, an audiobook or podcast on a sleep timer, a boring-but-pleasant mental task |
| **Hyperfocus** | Deep in a project, time vanishes, and suddenly it's 2 a.m. | An external stop signal: lights that dim on their own, an alarm in another room, a "parking note" for where you left off |
| **"Revenge bedtime"** | The night is the only time that feels like yours, so you stay up to claim it | Build some "mine" time into the day; give the night a cozy, low-light version of that time instead of cutting it |
| **Sensory noise** | Scratchy sheets, a ticking clock, light leaks, the wrong temperature | The sensory sanctuary in Week 8: blackout, white noise, cool room, sheets your skin likes, weighted blanket if it helps |
| **Body signals** | Hunger, needing the bathroom, or being too warm can be hard to notice until they wake you | A small snack and water nearby, a bathroom trip as part of wind-down, a fan |
| **Stimulants and caffeine** | ADHD meds and caffeine can keep the brain lit up if taken late | Talk to your prescriber about dose timing; give caffeine a cutoff about 8 hours before you want to sleep |
| **Snoring or gasping** | Sleep-disordered breathing (including sleep apnea) is more common with ADHD and can look like "bad sleep" or more ADHD symptoms | ⚠ Get checked by a doctor (see "When to get help" below) |

### The menu (pick any)

**Anchor the morning, not the night.** For PDA brains, a set bedtime can feel like a demand, and demands invite the brain to push back. A **steady wake-up time** works better as the anchor: the body clock sets itself from when you get up and see daylight. Bedtime then drifts toward the right spot on its own over a couple of weeks.

**Light is the clock's steering wheel.**

- **Morning:** get outside (or by a bright window) within an hour of waking for 10–30 minutes. Coffee on the porch or by the window counts.
- **Evening:** dim the lights 1–2 hours before you want to sleep. Warm, low lamps instead of overhead lights. Screens on night mode and dimmed, or swapped for audio.

```tip
**Smart-home tie-in (optional):** an automation that starts dimming the house lights and warming their color a couple of hours before your target sleep time, and turns the bedroom lamp up slowly at your wake time (a sunrise alarm). Nobody tells you it's bedtime; the house just gets cozy. A smart speaker can announce "wind-down mode" if you want a cue, or stay quiet if you don't.
```

**A wind-down, not a bedtime.** A short, predictable sequence tells the brain what's coming. Make it yours: shower, pajamas, pet the cat, brain dump, audiobook. Same order most nights is the useful part, not the time on the clock.

**The bed is for sleep.** If you're lying awake and frustrated for what feels like 20 minutes or so, it's an option to get up, go somewhere dim, do something calm and boring, and come back when you're sleepy. This is a core piece of CBT-I (below), and it trains the brain to link the bed with sleeping, not with lying there stewing. Optional, like everything here.

**For the racing brain:**

- **Brain dump:** a notebook by the bed. Write the thought down, and it's "parked" so the brain can let go of it.
- **Audio on a timer:** a calm audiobook or podcast you've heard before (familiar is better than gripping) with a sleep timer.
- **The boring mental task:** name an animal for every letter of the alphabet, or mentally walk through every room of a house you know well.
- **Body first:** slow breathing where the out-breath is longer than the in-breath, or tensing and releasing muscle groups from toes to head.

**Things that tend to backfire:** clock-watching (turn the clock away), "trying harder" to sleep, long naps late in the day, and making up for a bad night by sleeping hours past your anchor time.

### Medicines and supplements

- **Melatonin and other sleep supplements:** talk to a doctor or pharmacist before starting them, especially alongside other medications. In the US, supplements aren't regulated like medicines, so doses and quality vary between brands.
- **ADHD medication timing:** if your meds seem to affect your sleep, your prescriber can often adjust the timing or type. Don't change doses on your own.
- **Prescription sleep medicines:** a doctor's call. The American College of Physicians notes that medications should ideally be used for no longer than four to five weeks, while the skills learned in CBT-I can handle insomnia over the long term.

### ⚠ When to get help

Talk to a doctor if:

- Sleep trouble happens **most nights** and has lasted **more than a few weeks**, or it's hurting your work, mood, driving or safety.
- You **snore loudly**, someone sees you **stop breathing or gasp** in your sleep, or you wake with headaches or a dry mouth. Those can be signs of sleep apnea, which needs a sleep study.
- Your legs feel crawly or have to move in the evening (restless legs).
- You're **dozing off while driving** or in situations where you shouldn't. Pull over and don't drive drowsy.

**The best-supported treatment for chronic insomnia isn't a pill.** The American College of Physicians recommends **cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)** as the first-line treatment for adults with chronic insomnia. CBT-I combines cognitive therapy around sleep, behavioral techniques like stimulus control and sleep restriction, and sleep education. It can be done one-on-one, in groups, by phone or online, or with self-help books. If you go to a therapist or sleep clinic, it's fair to ask whether they're familiar with autistic and ADHD clients: a plan that respects sensory needs and demand avoidance works a lot better than a rigid one.

**Sleep restriction** (a CBT-I technique that temporarily limits time in bed) is powerful but can make you very sleepy at first. It's best done with a clinician guiding it, and **not** if you have seizures, bipolar disorder, or a job where drowsiness is dangerous, unless your doctor okays it.

## Quick Reference

### ⚠ Never mix

| Never mix | Makes |
| --- | --- |
| Bleach + ammonia (incl. urine, glass cleaners) | Chloramine gas |
| Bleach + any acid (vinegar, lemon, citrus vinegar, toilet and rust cleaners) | Chlorine gas |
| Bleach + rubbing alcohol | Chloroform and other toxic compounds |
| Hydrogen peroxide + vinegar (same container) | Peracetic acid (corrosive) |
| Two drain cleaners | Heat, spattering, fumes |

**Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222.** **ASPCA Animal Poison Control: (888) 426-4435.**

### What to use where

| Surface | Use | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ceramic or porcelain tile | Dish soap + water; baking soda paste on grout | Nothing major |
| Natural stone (marble, travertine, granite) | pH-neutral stone cleaner, mild soap | **Vinegar, lemon, citrus, acidic cleaners** (etching) |
| Vinyl / LVP | Damp mop, pH-neutral cleaner | Steam (unless approved), soaking, harsh abrasives |
| Hardwood / laminate | Dry mop; barely damp wood cleaner | Water puddles, steam, vinegar habit |
| Glass and mirrors | Squeegee; soapy water or half vinegar/half water | Direct sun while cleaning |
| Stainless steel | Damp cloth, wipe with the grain; drop of mineral oil to polish | Abrasive pads, bleach |
| Glass-top stove | Baking soda + hot towel; flat razor scraper | Abrasive scrubbers |
| Wood cutting board | Hot soapy water by hand; lemon and salt; mineral oil | Dishwasher, soaking |
| Reptile habitat | Unscented soap, then disinfectant; rinse and dry fully | Phenols, pine oil, essential oils, anything scented |
| Pet urine | Enzymatic cleaner, left wet | Ammonia, bleach, steam, heat |
